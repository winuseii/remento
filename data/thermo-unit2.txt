F: State the steady flow energy equation (SFEE) in rate form and per unit mass.
$$\left(\dot m h_1 + \dot m \frac{V_1^2}{2} + \dot m g z_1\right) + \dot Q = \left(\dot m h_2 + \dot m \frac{V_2^2}{2} + \dot m g z_2\right) + \dot W$$
$$h_1 + \frac{V_1^2}{2} + g z_1 + q = h_2 + \frac{V_2^2}{2} + g z_2 + w$$
sym: \dot m | mass flow rate | kg/s
sym: h | specific enthalpy | kJ/kg
sym: V | flow velocity | m/s
sym: z | elevation above datum | m
sym: \dot Q | rate of heat transfer into the CV | kW
sym: \dot W | rate of shaft work out of the CV | kW
trap: Dividing by mass flow gives the per-unit-mass form in kJ/kg. Mixing the two forms mid-solution is the most common way this equation goes wrong. Velocity terms are V²/2 in J/kg, so divide by 1000 before adding to h in kJ/kg.
src: Own notes p.21
rev: yes

L: State the two assumptions behind the steady flow energy equation.
- Mass transfer: by conservation of mass, mass accumulation inside the control volume Δm = 0, so ṁ_in = ṁ_out
- The properties throughout the control volume are constant with respect to time — that is, the flow is steady
trap: "Steady" constrains time, not space. Properties may still vary from inlet to exit; they just do not change at any fixed point as time passes.
src: Own notes p.21

F: State the continuity equation for steady flow through a control volume.
$$\rho_1 A_1 V_1 = \rho_2 A_2 V_2 \qquad \frac{A_1 V_1}{v_1} = \frac{A_2 V_2}{v_2}$$
sym: \rho | density | kg/m³
sym: A | cross-sectional area | m²
sym: V | velocity | m/s
sym: v | specific volume | m³/kg
trap: For an ideal gas it is more convenient to use the specific-volume form, since v = RT/P comes straight from the state equation. Your notes say exactly this.

Q: In an SFEE problem on a turbine, when can the inlet kinetic energy term be dropped, and when can it not?
A: It can be dropped when the inlet velocity is small compared with the exit velocity, which is the usual case for a turbine — your notes' worked example states "the inlet velocity is small compared to the exit velocity of 100 m/s" and drops it. It cannot be dropped for nozzles and diffusers, where the whole purpose of the device is to change velocity, so the kinetic energy terms are the dominant ones.
trap: Potential energy terms (gz) are almost always negligible and are stated as such; kinetic energy terms are only negligible for turbines, compressors and heat exchangers.
src: Own notes pp.22, 24
imp: 3

Q: For an ideal gas in an SFEE problem, why can you not use steam tables, and what do you use instead?
A: Air is not a pure substance with tabulated saturation properties in the same way, so enthalpy is obtained from the ideal gas relation instead: h = u + Pv = CvT + RT = (Cv + R)T = CpT. So h = Cp·T.
why: Your notes derive exactly this line before the air-turbine problem, because the instinct is to reach for the steam table.
trap: h = CpT requires T in kelvin, and it gives enthalpy relative to an arbitrary zero — fine for differences, meaningless as an absolute.
src: Own notes p.22
